Q.2 (i) 5 marks Asked in both papers

Define Biotransformation. Give difference between phase I and II reactions.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 1 (General Pharmacology, Pharmacotherapeutics, and Pharmacokinetics), pp. 42–46 · Katzung Ch. 4 (Drug Biotransformation), pp. 56–57, 63–64

Definition

  • Biotransformation (drug metabolism) is the chemical change of a drug inside the body, mostly by enzymes, in which lipid-soluble (lipophilic) drugs are transformed into more polar (water-soluble) products that are more readily excreted. (L 42–43; K 56)
  • Why it is needed: the kidney cannot efficiently excrete lipophilic drugs, because they cross cell membranes and are reabsorbed in the distal convoluted tubules. (L 43)
  • Main site: the liver. Other sites: gastrointestinal tract, lungs, skin, kidneys, brain. (K 57)

Difference between phase I and phase II

PointPhase IPhase II
What happensA polar functional group (–OH, –NH2, –SH) is introduced or unmasked. (L 43; K 57)Conjugation: the drug or its phase I metabolite is joined to an endogenous substance. (L 45; K 57)
ReactionsOxidation, reduction, hydrolysis. (L 44)Glucuronidation (most common and most important), sulfation, acetylation, amino acid (glycine) and glutathione conjugation, methylation. (L 45–46; K 64)
EnzymesMostly the cytochrome P450 (CYP) system, e.g. CYP3A4/5, CYP2D6. Some do not use P450, e.g. esterases, alcohol dehydrogenation. (L 44–45)Transferases in microsomes or cytosol, e.g. UDP-glucuronosyltransferase, sulfotransferase, N-acetyltransferase. (K 63–64)
Effect on activityMay increase, decrease, or not change activity. (L 44)Conjugate is usually inactive. Exception: morphine-6-glucuronide is more potent than morphine. (L 45)
ProductMore polar, but often still too lipophilic to be excreted. (L 45)Highly polar and water soluble; readily excreted in urine or bile. (L 45–46)
OrderUsually comes first. (L 44, Fig. 1.32)Usually follows phase I, but a drug that already has –OH, –NH2 or –COOH can enter phase II directly. (L 46)
Q.2 (ii) 5 marks Asked in both papers

What is loading dose of drug? How is it calculated?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 1, p. 51 · Katzung Ch. 3 (Pharmacokinetics & Pharmacodynamics: Rational Dosing & the Time Course of Drug Action), pp. 50–51

Definition

  • A loading dose is a dose given at the start of treatment to reach the desired (target) plasma level rapidly. It is followed by a maintenance dose, which keeps the steady state. (L 51; K 50)
  • Why it is needed: with maintenance doses alone it takes four to five half-lives to reach the steady-state concentration (Css). This is too slow for drugs with long half-lives. (L 51; K 50)
  • When it is used: when a rapid effect is needed, for example in serious infections, seizures, or arrhythmias. (L 51)

Calculation

Loading dose = Vd × desired steady-state plasma concentration ÷ F
  • Vd = apparent volume of distribution; F = bioavailability (fraction absorbed). (L 42, 51)
  • For intravenous use the bioavailability is 100% (F = 1), so: Loading dose = Vd × desired plasma concentration. (L 51)
  • Katzung writes the same thing as: Loading dose = V × TC (volume of distribution × target concentration). (K 51)
  • Example: theophylline, target 10 mg/L, V = 35 L in a 70-kg person. Loading dose = 35 L × 10 mg/L = 350 mg. (K 51)
  • The loading dose depends on the volume of distribution. The maintenance dose depends on clearance. (K 50; L 51)

Disadvantages

  • Increased risk of drug toxicity, and a longer time for the plasma level to fall if it goes too high. (L 51)
  • After a rapid intravenous loading dose the plasma level can at first be much higher than wanted (e.g. lidocaine), so it should be injected slowly, over minutes rather than seconds. (K 51)
Q.2 (iii) 5 marks

Enumerate first line drugs used to treat tuberculosis? What is their mechanism of action?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 32 (Antimycobacterial Drugs), pp. 551–555 · Katzung Ch. 47 (Antimycobacterial Drugs), pp. 842–843, 846

First-line drugs

  1. Isoniazid (INH)
  2. Rifampin (rifabutin or rifapentine may replace rifampin in some cases)
  3. Pyrazinamide
  4. Ethambutol

These four are preferred because of their high efficacy and acceptable toxicity. (L 551–552; K 842–843) Both books place streptomycin with the second-line drugs. (L 556; K 843)

Mechanism of action

DrugMechanism of action
IsoniazidA prodrug, activated by a mycobacterial catalase–peroxidase (KatG). It targets the enzymes acyl carrier protein reductase (InhA) and β-ketoacyl-ACP synthase (KasA), which are essential for making mycolic acid. Without mycolic acid the bacterial cell wall is disrupted. (L 552)
RifampinBinds the β subunit of mycobacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase and blocks RNA transcription. Bactericidal for intracellular and extracellular mycobacteria. (L 553)
PyrazinamidePrecise mechanism is unclear. It must be hydrolyzed by the enzyme pyrazinamidase to pyrazinoic acid, the active form. Active against bacilli in acidic lesions and in macrophages. (L 554–555) Pyrazinoic acid disrupts mycobacterial cell membrane metabolism and transport functions. (K 846)
EthambutolInhibits arabinosyl transferase, an enzyme important for synthesis of the mycobacterial cell wall. Bacteriostatic and specific for mycobacteria. (L 555)
  • How they are used: all four for 2 months (intensive phase), then isoniazid and rifampin for 4 months (continuation phase). Several drugs are always given together to stop resistant organisms emerging. (L 552)
Q.2 (iv) 5 marks

A young athlete has used androgens to enhance her performance in competitive sports. What is the mechanism of action of this drug? What will be the adverse effects of this drug on her?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 25 (Estrogens and Androgens), pp. 466–468 · Katzung Ch. 40 (The Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors), pp. 741–742

Mechanism of action

  • Androgens (testosterone and its derivatives, the anabolic steroids) are steroids with anabolic and/or masculinizing effects in both males and females. (L 466)
  • The androgen enters the target cell and binds to a specific nuclear (intracellular) androgen receptor. (L 467; K 741)
  • In muscle and liver, testosterone itself is the active ligand. In skin, prostate, seminal vesicles and epididymis it is first converted by 5α-reductase to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which binds the receptor. (L 467)
  • Receptor binding leads to growth, differentiation, and synthesis of enzymes and other functional proteins. (K 741)
  • Result: increased synthesis of muscle proteins and hemoglobin, and less nitrogen excretion (a sign of protein building). This effect is more marked in women. (L 467; K 741)
  • This is why athletes misuse them: to increase lean body mass, muscle strength and endurance (an unapproved use). (L 467)

Adverse effects on her

  • Masculinization: acne, growth of facial hair (hirsutism), deepening of the voice, male pattern baldness, excessive muscle development, enlargement of the clitoris. (L 468; K 741–742)
  • Menstrual irregularities, amenorrhea. (L 468; K 742)
  • Growth: in a young athlete, premature closing of the epiphyses of the long bones, which stunts growth. (L 468)
  • Lipids: raised LDL and lowered HDL. (L 468)
  • Fluid retention and peripheral edema. (L 468)
  • Liver: hepatic abnormalities, especially with the oral 17α-alkylated androgens. (L 468)
  • Behavior: increased aggression (“roid rage”) and major mood disorders. (L 468)
  • If she becomes pregnant: virilization of a female fetus. (L 468)

Q.2 (vi) 5 marks

What is mechanism of action of tetracyclines? Why tetracycline are not prescribed in children?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 30 (Protein Synthesis Inhibitors), pp. 521–524 · Katzung Ch. 44 (Tetracyclines, Macrolides, Clindamycin, Chloramphenicol, Streptogramins, & Oxazolidinones), pp. 817–818

Mechanism of action

  • Tetracyclines (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline) are protein synthesis inhibitors. (L 521)
  • Entry: they enter susceptible bacteria by passive diffusion and by an energy-dependent transport protein found only in the bacterial inner cytoplasmic membrane. They concentrate inside the organism. (L 521)
  • Binding: they bind reversibly to the 30S subunit of the bacterial ribosome. (L 521)
  • Effect: this prevents binding of tRNA to the mRNA–ribosome complex, so bacterial protein synthesis is inhibited. (L 521)
  • They are bacteriostatic and broad spectrum. (L 521)

Why they are not prescribed in children

  • Tetracyclines bind to tissues that are undergoing calcification, that is, growing teeth and bones. (L 523) This happens because they chelate with calcium. (K 817)
  • In growing children the drug is deposited in bone and primary dentition during calcification. (L 524)
  • Teeth: discoloration and hypoplasia of the teeth (enamel dysplasia). (L 524; K 818)
  • Bone: temporary stunting of growth; deformity or growth inhibition. (L 524; K 818)
  • So tetracyclines are contraindicated in children under 8 years. (L 524)
  • For the same reason they are avoided in pregnant and breast-feeding women: they cross the placenta and concentrate in fetal bones and teeth. (L 524)

Q.2 (viii) 5 marks

Why propranolol should not be prescribed to a young athlete?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 7 (Adrenergic Antagonists), pp. 170–172, 174; Ch. 16 (Antihypertensives), p. 317 (Fig. 16.9)

Note: Lippincott lists “athletes and physically active patients” as a possible contraindication to β-blockers, and “fatigue, decreased exercise tolerance” among their adverse effects (Fig. 16.9, p. 317). It does not explain why in one place. The reasons below are the book's own facts about propranolol, applied to an athlete.

  • Propranolol is a non-selective β-blocker. It blocks both β1 and β2 receptors. (L 170)
  • Heart (β1 block): it lowers heart rate and force of contraction, so cardiac output falls. During exercise it blunts the expected rise in heart rate. The athlete's heart cannot raise its output to meet the demand of exercise. (L 170)
  • Muscle blood flow (β2 block): it prevents β2-mediated vasodilation in skeletal muscle. Reflex peripheral vasoconstriction further reduces blood flow to the periphery. Working muscles get less blood. (L 170)
  • Fuel supply: it decreases glycogenolysis and glucagon secretion, and β receptors are needed to mobilize free fatty acids. Less glucose and fat are available as energy during exercise. Fasting hypoglycemia may occur, and its warning symptoms (tremor, tachycardia) are blunted. (L 171–172)
  • Airways (β2 block): bronchoconstriction. This can be dangerous if the athlete has asthma. (L 171–172)
  • CNS effects: fatigue, lethargy, weakness and dizziness. (L 172)
  • Overall result: fatigue and decreased exercise tolerance, so performance falls. (L 317)
  • If a β-blocker is needed, a cardioselective (β1-selective) drug such as atenolol or metoprolol has fewer of these effects. (L 174)
Q.3 15 marks Asked in both papers

What different techniques are available and how they enhance the transcutaneous penetration of drugs? What drugs can be used? Compare the advantages and disadvantages of these methodologies with the oral route of administration.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 1, pp. 29, 31–32 (Fig. 1.19), 36, and transdermal examples on pp. 139, 189, 221, 288, 326, 459, 467, 698 · Katzung Ch. 3, pp. 47–48; Ch. 61 (Dermatologic Pharmacology), pp. 1068–1069

Note: this is mainly a physical-therapy topic and the two books cover it only partly. They describe the transdermal route, how drugs are absorbed through skin, and the oral route. Iontophoresis is only named once in Katzung (p. 115, as a way of applying a drug in an experiment) and is not in Lippincott. Phonophoresis is in neither book. Everything about those two techniques is tagged.

1. The skin as a barrier

  • Skin is a series of diffusion barriers. A drug must leave its vehicle, diffuse through the stratum corneum (the outer layer), pass into the deeper layers and dermis, and is then absorbed into the bloodstream. (K 1068–1069, Fig. 61–1)
  • The transdermal route gives systemic effects by applying the drug to the skin, usually as a patch. (L 31)

2. Techniques, and how each one enhances penetration

TechniqueHow it enhances penetration
Transdermal patchA drug reservoir, a drug-release membrane and an adhesive hold the drug against the skin. Absorption is slow and sustained. (L 31–32)
OcclusionA plastic wrap holds the drug and vehicle in close contact with the skin. Extremely effective. (K 1068)
Choice of vehicleA suitable vehicle helps the drug penetrate the outer layers of the skin. A vehicle that hydrates the stratum corneum enhances penetration; ointments retard evaporation from the skin the most. (K 1068–1069)
Higher concentrationA bigger concentration gradient moves more drug across per unit time. (K 1068)
Choice of siteScrotum, face, axilla and scalp are far more permeable than the forearm. (K 1068)
IontophoresisA low-level direct electric current pushes an ionized drug through the skin. Like charges repel: a positive drug is placed under the positive electrode (anode), a negative drug under the negative electrode (cathode). [not in book]
Phonophoresis (sonophoresis)Therapeutic ultrasound drives a drug mixed in the coupling gel through the skin. Heating and mechanical effects (acoustic streaming, cavitation) make the stratum corneum more permeable. [not in book]

3. Drugs that can be used

  • What the drug must be like: highly lipophilic, and effective in small daily doses. Drugs with high molecular weight or poor lipid solubility are unsuitable. (L 32)
  • Transdermal patches: nitroglycerin, nicotine, scopolamine (L 32); fentanyl (L 288); clonidine (L 326); estradiol (L 459); testosterone (L 467); rotigotine (L 189); selegiline (L 221); oxybutynin (L 139).
  • Topical gels and ointments for painful joints and muscles: diclofenac, piroxicam, ibuprofen, nimesulide, methyl salicylate. (L 698)
  • Iontophoresis: dexamethasone, lidocaine, salicylates, acetic acid, zinc oxide, iodine. [not in book]
  • Phonophoresis: hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, salicylates, lidocaine, NSAID gels. [not in book]

4. Comparison with the oral route

Through the skin (transcutaneous)Oral route
Advantages
  • Bypasses the first-pass effect. (L 32; K 48)
  • Convenient and painless. (L 32)
  • Ideal for lipophilic drugs with poor oral bioavailability. (L 32)
  • Slow, sustained absorption and prolonged action; ideal for drugs that are quickly eliminated. (L 32; K 47)
  • Local application gives a high concentration at the site and little elsewhere. (K 48)
  • Iontophoresis and phonophoresis: no needle, drug is delivered to the target tissue, and gastric irritation is avoided. [not in book]
  • Safest, most common, most convenient and most economical route. (L 32)
  • Easily self-administered. (L 29)
  • Overdose can be treated with antidotes such as activated charcoal. (L 29)
Disadvantages
  • Drug must be highly lipophilic. (L 32)
  • Only for drugs given in small daily doses. (L 32)
  • Delayed delivery to the site of action. (L 32)
  • Absorption varies with the skin at the site. (L 31)
  • Patch allergy and skin irritation. (L 32)
  • Iontophoresis and phonophoresis: need equipment and a trained therapist, take treatment time, and deliver only small amounts. Iontophoresis works only for ionized drugs and the current can burn or irritate the skin. [not in book]
  • First-pass metabolism: drug may be metabolized in the gut wall or liver before it reaches the circulation. (L 32, 36)
  • Limited absorption of some drugs. (L 32)
  • Food may affect absorption. (L 32)
  • Low gastric pH inactivates some drugs. (L 29)
  • Patient compliance is necessary. (L 32)
Q.4 5 + 5 + 5 marks Adverse effects asked in both papers

A 14 years old female patient suffering from bronchial asthma is on self-medication with corticosteroids (both oral and by inhalation) for the last many years. What is the mechanism of action and adverse effects of long term use of corticosteroids? Also enumerate different names of these drugs.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 26 (Adrenal Hormones), pp. 471–476; Ch. 41 (Drugs for Disorders of the Respiratory System), pp. 724–725 · Katzung Ch. 39 (Adrenocorticosteroids & Adrenocortical Antagonists), pp. 712–713; Ch. 20 (Drugs Used in Asthma), p. 355

(a) Mechanism of action (5)

  1. The steroid is lipid soluble. It diffuses across the cell membrane and binds to a specific intracellular (cytoplasmic) receptor. (L 471, 473)
  2. The receptor–hormone complex forms a dimer and moves into the nucleus. (L 471)
  3. There it attaches to gene promoter elements (glucocorticoid response elements) and acts as a transcription factor, turning genes on or off. (L 471, 473)
  4. The amounts of specific proteins change, and this gives the effect. Because of this, some effects take hours to days. (L 471, 473)

Anti-inflammatory actions: (L 472)

  • Inhibit phospholipase A2, so arachidonic acid is not released and prostaglandins and leukotrienes are not formed.
  • Decrease the production and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines.
  • Lower circulating lymphocytes and stop leukocytes and macrophages responding to antigens.
  • Stabilize mast cell and basophil membranes, so less histamine is released.

In asthma: inhaled corticosteroids reduce airway inflammation (fewer eosinophils, macrophages and T lymphocytes), reverse mucosal edema, decrease capillary permeability and inhibit leukotriene release. After months of regular use the airway becomes less hyper-responsive. (L 724)

Q.5 4 + 4 + 7 marks

Classify NSAIDS. What is their mechanism of action? Enumerate their adverse effects on gastric mucosa, kidney and cardiovascular system.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 40 (Anti-inflammatory, Antipyretic, and Analgesic Agents), pp. 693–694 (Fig. 40.1), 696–697, 699–700 · Katzung Ch. 36 (NSAIDs, DMARDs, Nonopioid Analgesics, & Drugs Used in Gout), p. 645

(a) Classification (4)

NSAIDs = nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. COX = cyclooxygenase. (L 693–694, 696)

GroupDrugs

Diclofenac, aceclofenac, nimesulide, meloxicam, etodolac

Celecoxib, etoricoxib, parecoxib (printed “paricoxib” in Lippincott)

(b) Mechanism of action (4)

  • Arachidonic acid is released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2. The cyclooxygenase pathway turns it into prostaglandins, thromboxanes and prostacyclin. (L 694)
  • NSAIDs inhibit the cyclooxygenase enzymes, which catalyze the first step of this pathway. Prostaglandin synthesis falls. (L 696)
  • COX-1 is constitutive: it serves normal functions such as gastric protection, platelet aggregation and kidney function. COX-2 is increased at sites of inflammation. (L 694)
  • Aspirin acetylates COX irreversibly. The other NSAIDs are reversible inhibitors. (L 697)
  • Three actions result: (L 697)
    • Anti-inflammatory: fewer prostaglandins to drive inflammation.
    • Analgesic: less PGE2, which normally sensitizes nerve endings to bradykinin and histamine.
    • Antipyretic: less PGE2 in the hypothalamus, so the raised temperature set point returns towards normal.
  • COX-2 inhibition gives the anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. COX-1 inhibition gives most of the adverse effects. (L 696)

(c) Adverse effects (7)

Gastric mucosa (the most common adverse effects) (L 699; K 645)

  • Dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain.
  • Gastric ulceration.
  • Gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Why: prostacyclin (PGI2) normally inhibits gastric acid secretion, and PGE2 and PGF2α stimulate protective mucus. COX-1 inhibition removes these, so acid rises and mucus protection falls.
  • Risk is higher with COX-1-selective drugs and lower with COX-2-selective drugs.
  • Prevention: take with food; add a proton-pump inhibitor or misoprostol in high-risk patients.

Kidney (L 700; K 645)

  • PGE2 and PGI2 maintain renal blood flow. NSAIDs block their synthesis, so renal blood flow falls.
  • Retention of sodium and water; edema.
  • Acute kidney injury in susceptible patients (heart failure, kidney disease).
  • Renal insufficiency, renal failure, hyperkalemia, proteinuria.
  • Reduced effect of antihypertensive drugs.

Cardiovascular system (L 700; K 645)

  • Increased risk of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke, with all NSAIDs except aspirin.
  • The risk is linked to COX-2 selectivity, possibly because less PGI2 is made.
  • Fluid retention, edema and hypertension; rarely, congestive heart failure.
  • Increased bleeding: aspirin stops platelets making thromboxane A2 for the life of the platelet, so bleeding time is prolonged. Other NSAIDs can also prolong bleeding time.
  • Other NSAIDs can stop aspirin binding to COX, and so reduce its protective effect on the heart.

  • Hydrochlorothiazide is a thiazide diuretic. It acts mainly in the distal convoluted tubule. (L 334)

  • Heart failure: added when the patient is resistant to loop diuretics.
  • Hypercalciuria and calcium oxalate stones in the urinary tract.
  • Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

Adverse effects (L 336)

  • Hypokalemia (the most frequent)
  • Hyponatremia
  • Hypomagnesemia
  • Hyperuricemia (may precipitate gout)
  • Hypercalcemia
  • Hyperglycemia
  • Hypovolemia: orthostatic hypotension, light-headedness

Short Q.9 5 marks (assumed)

Write down mechanism of action of aminoglycosides with specific adverse effects.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 30 (Protein Synthesis Inhibitors), pp. 521, 525–527

Drugs: gentamicin, amikacin, tobramycin, streptomycin, neomycin. (L 521)

Mechanism of action (L 525–526)

  • Entry: they diffuse through porin channels in the outer membrane of the bacterium. An oxygen-dependent system then carries them across the cytoplasmic membrane. This is why they do not work in anaerobic conditions.
  • Binding: inside the cell they bind the 30S ribosomal subunit.
  • Effect: they interfere with assembly of the working ribosome and/or make the 30S subunit misread the genetic code. Protein synthesis fails.
  • They are bactericidal, and the killing is concentration dependent.
  • They have a post-antibiotic effect: bacteria stay suppressed after the drug level falls below the minimum inhibitory concentration. So a high dose can be given at long intervals.

Specific adverse effects (L 527)

  • Ototoxicity (vestibular and auditory): the drug accumulates in the endolymph and perilymph of the inner ear. Deafness may be irreversible. Vertigo occurs especially with streptomycin. Risk is higher with other ototoxic drugs such as loop diuretics or cisplatin.
  • Nephrotoxicity: the drug is retained by proximal tubular cells. Damage ranges from mild, reversible renal impairment to severe acute tubular necrosis.
  • Neuromuscular paralysis: with high doses infused quickly, or given with neuromuscular blockers. Patients with myasthenia gravis are at particular risk. Reversed by calcium gluconate or neostigmine.
  • Allergic reactions: contact dermatitis with topical neomycin.
  • Pregnancy: they cross the placenta and can cause fetal ototoxicity, so they are contraindicated.
Short Q.10 5 marks (assumed)

What is mechanism of action and clinical uses of benzodiazepines.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 9 (Anxiolytic/Hypnotic Drugs), pp. 197–200

Mechanism of action (L 197–198)

  • The target is the GABAA receptor. GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) is the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS.
  • The GABAA receptor is made of five subunits (α, β and γ) around a central chloride ion channel.

By duration of action: short: procaine, chloroprocaine. Intermediate: lidocaine, mepivacaine. Long: bupivacaine, ropivacaine, tetracaine. (L 277)

(b) Mechanism of action

  • Local anesthetics block sodium ion channels in the nerve membrane. (L 274)
  • This prevents the brief rise in Na+ permeability that is needed for an action potential. (L 274)
  • The action potential cannot be propagated, so sensation is not carried from the site to the brain. (L 274)
  • They are weak bases. The non-ionized form is lipophilic and diffuses through the nerve membrane. Once inside, the ionized form binds the receptor of the Na+ channel and blocks it. (L 274, 276)
  • Small, unmyelinated fibers for pain, temperature and autonomic activity are the most sensitive. Higher concentrations also block motor impulses. (L 273, 275)
  • Onset depends mainly on pKa: a lower pKa means more non-ionized drug and a quicker onset. In infected tissue the pH is low, so onset is delayed or prevented. (L 276)
  • Potency and duration depend mainly on lipid solubility. (L 276)
  • They cause vasodilation, which carries the drug away. Epinephrine (a vasoconstrictor) is added to slow absorption, prolong the action and reduce systemic toxicity. (L 276)

(c) Adverse effects

1. Systemic toxicity (from repeated injections or accidental intravenous injection) (L 277; K 467)

  • CNS: numbness around the mouth and of the tongue, metallic taste (early signs); light-headedness, restlessness, visual and hearing disturbances; nystagmus and muscle twitching; tonic-clonic convulsions; then generalized CNS depression. (K 467)
  • Heart: disturbed cardiac conduction and function, arrhythmias, cardiac arrest. Bupivacaine has greater cardiotoxicity than lidocaine. (K 468)
  • Treatment: control seizures, manage the airway, support the circulation, and give a 20% lipid emulsion infusion. (L 277)

2. Allergic reactions (L 276–277)

  • More common with esters, because all esters are broken down to PABA. Allergy to one ester rules out the others.
  • True allergy to an amide is very rare.

3. Methemoglobinemia

  • With prilocaine (from a metabolite) and benzocaine. (L 276; K 470)

4. Local nerve toxicity (K 468–469)

  • Neural injury after spinal anesthesia (e.g. cauda equina syndrome).
  • Transient neurologic symptoms (pain or dysesthesia) after spinal lidocaine.

5. Complications of spinal anesthesia (L 275)

  • Hypotension, bradycardia, headache, nausea and vomiting, respiratory paralysis, septic meningitis.

6. From added epinephrine (L 276)

  • Ischemia and necrosis if used in fingers and toes (end arteries). Caution in cardiovascular disease and thyrotoxicosis.

What it is

  • Iontophoresis is the delivery of an ionized drug through the skin using a low-level direct electric current. [not in book]
  • The barrier it has to overcome is the stratum corneum, the outer layer of the skin, which normally only lets small lipophilic drugs through. (K 1068–1069; L 32)

A. Mechanism of delivery of drugs

  • Like charges repel (electrorepulsion). The drug is in solution as ions and is placed under the electrode with the same charge. The current pushes the ions away from that electrode and into the skin. [not in book]
  • Positive drug ions go under the positive electrode (anode). Negative drug ions go under the negative electrode (cathode). A second (dispersive) electrode elsewhere on the skin completes the circuit. [not in book]
  • Electro-osmosis: the current also moves water through the skin, and this flow carries drug with it. [not in book]
  • The current itself makes the skin more permeable. The ions pass mainly through sweat ducts and hair follicles. [not in book]
  • Dose = current × time (milliampere-minutes). A stronger current or a longer time delivers more drug. [not in book]
  • Once through the stratum corneum, the drug moves into the deeper layers and dermis and is absorbed into local tissue and the bloodstream, as for any drug applied to skin. (K 1069, Fig. 61–1)

Paper 1 · Q.3 Transcutaneous penetration techniques
