Q.2 (1) 6 marks

Compare and contrast tolerance and tachyphylaxis with examples in tabulated form.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 2 (Pharmacodynamics), p. 61; Ch. 9 (Anxiolytic/Hypnotic Drugs), pp. 199, 202, 205; Ch. 14 (Opioid Analgesics), p. 286; Ch. 20 (Antianginal Drugs), p. 386 · Katzung Ch. 2, p. 38; Ch. 9, p. 141; Ch. 12, p. 200; Ch. 20, p. 351; Ch. 22, pp. 389, 393; Ch. 31, p. 565; Ch. 32, p. 576

Definitions

  • Tolerance: decreased responsiveness to a drug following repeated exposure, so a larger dose is needed to get the same effect. (K 389; L 68) Lippincott: decreased responsiveness to repeated doses that occurs when a drug is used for more than 1 to 2 weeks. (L 199)
  • Tachyphylaxis: the response diminishes rapidly after the drug is given. (K 38) The receptor becomes desensitized by too much agonist stimulation, so the response falls. (L 61)

Comparison

FeatureToleranceTachyphylaxis
SpeedDevelops slowly, over days to weeks; opioid tolerance becomes clinically obvious after 2–3 weeks. (K 576, 565; L 199)Develops rapidly, within minutes to hours. (K 38, 141)
MechanismPharmacokinetic (metabolic): faster metabolism of the drug, e.g. barbiturates. Pharmacodynamic: changed responsiveness of the CNS or receptors, e.g. down-regulation of benzodiazepine receptors. (K 389, 576)Receptor desensitization: phosphorylation makes the receptor unresponsive; receptors may also be taken into the cell (down-regulation). (L 61; K 141)
Larger doseEffect is regained by increasing the dose. (K 576, 389)Increasing the dose does not restore the response. [not in book] Sensitivity returns after a drug-free interval. (L 386)
Related drugsCross-tolerance, e.g. benzodiazepines with alcohol and barbiturates. (L 202; K 393)Usually limited to the receptors exposed to the agonist (homologous desensitization). (K 141)
Examples
  • Morphine: to analgesia, euphoria, sedation and respiratory depression, but not to miosis or constipation. (L 286)
  • Benzodiazepines: to sedative, hypnotic and anticonvulsant effects; less to the anxiolytic effect. (L 202)
  • Barbiturates (L 205); alcohol, by enzyme induction (L 828).
  • Nitrates given continuously for more than a few hours. (K 200; L 386)
  • β2 agonists in asthma (K 351); β stimulants such as dobutamine (K 219); hydralazine (K 184).
  • LSD and other hallucinogens (K 582; L 826); flushing with niacin (K 636).
  • Ephedrine and tyramine, by using up stored noradrenaline. [not in book]
Clinical pointDose keeps rising; risk of overdose because respiratory depression shows less tolerance than the wanted effect. Dependence and withdrawal can follow. (K 576; L 202)Limits the therapeutic response. (K 141) With nitrates it is prevented by a daily nitrate-free interval of 10–12 hours. (L 386)
  • Similarity: both are a fall in response to a drug given repeatedly, and both can follow down-regulation or desensitization of receptors. (K 38)
  • The books use the words loosely: Katzung calls nitrate tolerance “complete tolerance (tachyphylaxis)” (K 200), while Lippincott calls it tolerance (L 386).
Q.2 (2) 6 marks

Define plasma half life and how will you calculate it?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 1 (General Pharmacology, Pharmacotherapeutics, and Pharmacokinetics), pp. 42–43 (Figs. 1.30, 1.31), 48–49, 699–700 · Katzung Ch. 3 (Pharmacokinetics & Pharmacodynamics), pp. 43, 45–46 (Fig. 3–3)

Definition

  • Plasma half-life (t½) is the time taken for the plasma concentration of a drug (or the amount of drug in the body) to fall to one-half during elimination. (L 42, 48; K 46)
  • It applies to drugs eliminated by first-order kinetics, where a fixed proportion of the drug is eliminated per unit time. (L 48)
  • It does not depend on the dose or the dosing interval. (L 48)

How it is calculated

1. From volume of distribution and clearance

t½ = 0.693 × Vd ÷ CL
  • Vd = apparent volume of distribution; CL = clearance; 0.693 = natural log of 2. (L 42) Katzung writes it as t½ = 0.7 × V ÷ CL. (K 46)
  • So a larger Vd or a smaller clearance gives a longer half-life. (L 42)
  • Example: digoxin, V = 500 L, CL = 9 L/h. t½ = 0.7 × 500 ÷ 9 ≈ 39 hours, the value in Katzung's table. (K 43, 46)

2. From a plasma concentration–time graph

  • Give the drug IV, measure plasma concentration at intervals, and plot log concentration against time. The elimination phase is a straight line. Read off the time taken for any concentration to fall to half (e.g. 4 → 2 mg/L). (L 42, Fig. 1.30)
  • Worked example: if the level falls from 80 to 40 mg/L in 6 hours, t½ = 6 hours.
  • From the elimination rate constant: t½ = 0.693 ÷ kel. [not in book]

Points to add

  • After 1, 2 and 3 half-lives, 50%, 75% and 87.5% of the drug is gone. After 4–5 half-lives it is almost eliminated, and the same 4–5 half-lives are needed to reach steady state. (L 48–49; K 46)
  • Zero order: enzymes are saturated, so a fixed amount is eliminated per unit time (ethanol, phenytoin, high-dose aspirin). (L 43; K 46) Half-life is then not constant: aspirin t½ is about 3.5 h at low doses but 15 h or more at anti-inflammatory doses. (L 699–700)
  • Uses of t½: choosing the dose and dosing frequency, and understanding the time course of adverse effects. (L 48) It is longer in renal or hepatic disease and heart failure, which need lower or less frequent doses. (L 48–49)

Q.2 (4) 3 + 3 marks

A 65 year old male complains of chest pain which is diffuse, sometimes radiates to the jaw. This pain is severe when he walks upstairs and is relieved on rest. Physician prescribes him nitrates to relieve his pain. Write in tabulated form the effect of nitrates alone and in presence of beta blockers. (3+3)

Source: Lippincott Ch. 20 (Antianginal Drugs), pp. 381–386 · Katzung Ch. 12 (Vasodilators & the Treatment of Angina Pectoris), pp. 199, 201, 206–209 (Table 12–7, p. 209)

  • Pain on exertion that radiates to the jaw and is relieved by rest is stable (effort) angina: a fixed coronary narrowing cannot meet the higher O2 demand of exercise. (L 381–382) Treatment aims to lower myocardial O2 demand. (L 382)

(a) Effect of nitrates alone (3)

  • Mechanism: nitrate → nitrite → nitric oxide → activates guanylyl cyclase → ↑ cGMP → myosin light chain dephosphorylated → vascular smooth muscle relaxes. (L 385)
  • Veins dilate most → less venous return (preload) → smaller ventricular volume and wall tension → less myocardial O2 demand, the main way nitrates relieve effort angina. (L 386; K 201) Arterial pressure also falls, and large epicardial coronary arteries dilate. (K 201; L 386)
  • Undesirable: the fall in blood pressure sets off a baroreceptor reflex → tachycardia and increased contractility, which raise O2 demand. (K 199, 201) Also headache, postural hypotension, flushing. (L 386)

(b) In the presence of β-blockers (3)

  • β-blockers block β1 receptors → lower heart rate, contractility and blood pressure → less O2 demand at rest and on exertion. (L 383; K 206)
  • Given alone they increase end-diastolic volume and ejection time, which raise O2 demand. (K 206–207)
  • Together each drug cancels the other's unwanted effect: the β-blocker stops the nitrate's reflex tachycardia, and the nitrate's venodilation stops the rise in end-diastolic volume. (K 207, 209) Cardiac work falls more than with either drug alone. (K 208)

  • For his attacks: sublingual nitroglycerin, onset 1–3 min. Long-acting nitrates need a 10–12 h nitrate-free interval to prevent tolerance; never combine with sildenafil (dangerous hypotension). (L 386) Do not stop a β-blocker suddenly (rebound angina). (L 384)
Q.2 (5) 6 marks Repeated topic

An epileptic lady had an attack of seizures was admitted in emergency. She was given injection of Diazepam to control her seizures. What would be possible mechanism of action and adverse effects of Diazepam?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 9 (Anxiolytic/Hypnotic Drugs), pp. 197–203; Ch. 12 (Drugs for Epilepsy), p. 252 · Katzung Ch. 22 (Sedative-Hypnotic Drugs), pp. 386–389, 392; Ch. 24 (Antiseizure Drugs), pp. 432–433; Ch. 25 (General Anesthetics), p. 454

Mechanism of action

  1. Diazepam is a long-acting benzodiazepine. (L 197) Its target is the GABAA receptor, a chloride channel made of five subunits (α, β, γ). GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter of the CNS. (L 197)
  2. When GABA binds, the channel opens, Cl⁻ flows in, the neuron is hyperpolarized and fires fewer action potentials. (L 198)
  1. Diazepam binds its own high-affinity site at the α/γ subunit interface, separate from the GABA site. (L 198; K 386)
  2. It does not open the channel by itself. It enhances GABA allosterically by increasing the frequency of channel opening. (L 198; K 387) (Barbiturates instead prolong the duration of opening. K 387)
  3. Anticonvulsant effect: raises the seizure threshold and stops the spread of epileptiform activity in the brain. (L 200; K 389)
  4. Why IV diazepam here: IV diazepam (or lorazepam) is first-line for status epilepticus. It enters the brain quickly but then redistributes into fat, so brain levels fall; a longer-acting drug such as phenytoin or fosphenytoin follows. (K 432–433; L 200, 252)

Adverse effects

SystemAdverse effects
CNS (most common)Drowsiness, sedation, confusion; ataxia and poor motor coordination; anterograde amnesia and cognitive impairment; hangover, because of a long half-life (20–80 h) and active metabolites. (L 198, 201–202; K 392) Elderly patients are more sensitive. (K 392)
RespirationRespiratory depression at high doses, worse with COPD or sleep apnea and more marked when given IV; added depression with alcohol or other CNS depressants. (L 202; K 389, 454)
Heart and vesselsHypotension and bradycardia when given parenterally to patients with impaired cardiovascular function. (L 203)
Injection sitePain on IV or IM injection and thrombophlebitis, because diazepam is poorly water-soluble and needs an organic solvent. (K 454)
ParadoxicalParadoxical excitement (L 203); hyperactivity in children (K 432).
Long-term useTolerance (seizures may return within months); psychological and physical dependence; withdrawal with anxiety, agitation, insomnia and, rarely, seizures. (K 432; L 202)
PregnancyCrosses the placenta and may depress the newborn; passes into breast milk. (L 202)
InteractionsCYP3A4 inhibitors and grapefruit juice prolong its action. (L 203)
  • Use with caution in liver disease, because clearance is reduced. (L 202)
Q.2 (6) 6 marks

Write down the mechanism of action and adverse effects of Omeprazole.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 42 (Gastrointestinal and Antiemetic Drugs), pp. 739–741 (Fig. 42.5) · Katzung Ch. 62 (Drugs Used in the Treatment of Gastrointestinal Diseases), pp. 1091–1095

Mechanism of action

  1. Omeprazole is a proton-pump inhibitor (PPI), a substituted benzimidazole. (L 740; K 1091)
  2. The proton pump, H⁺/K⁺-ATPase of the parietal cell, is the final step of gastric acid secretion, whatever the stimulus (histamine, acetylcholine or gastrin). (L 739–740; K 1093)
  1. It is an inactive prodrug with an acid-resistant enteric coating; the coating dissolves in the alkaline duodenum and the drug is absorbed. (L 740; K 1091)
  2. A weak base, it collects more than 1000-fold in the acidic canaliculus of the parietal cell, where it is converted to its active form (a sulfenamide). (K 1092)
  3. The active form makes a covalent (disulfide) bond with H⁺/K⁺-ATPase and irreversibly inactivates the pump. (K 1092; L 740)
  4. Result: basal and stimulated acid secretion falls by more than 90% (90–98% of 24-hour secretion). (L 740; K 1093)
  5. Plasma half-life is short (about 1 h) but the effect lasts up to 24 h, because new pumps take about 18 h to be made; full effect takes 3–4 days. (L 740–741; K 1092)
  6. Taken 30–60 min before breakfast, because only actively secreting pumps are blocked. (L 741; K 1092)

Uses, in one line: GERD, peptic ulcer, H. pylori eradication, prevention of NSAID ulcers, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, stress ulcer prophylaxis. (L 740)

Adverse effects

GroupAdverse effects
CommonDiarrhea, headache, abdominal pain. (K 1094)
Nutrition and boneLow vitamin B12 (acid is needed to free it from food); poor absorption of iron and calcium carbonate; hypomagnesemia; more hip, spine and wrist fractures, especially with use for a year or more. (L 741; K 1094)
InfectionsClostridium difficile diarrhea, pneumonia and other enteric infections, because gastric acid is a barrier to bacteria. (L 741; K 1094)
High gastrinSerum gastrin rises; ECL-cell hyperplasia; rebound acid hypersecretion after stopping; benign fundic-gland polyps. (K 1094–1095)
Kidney and brainAcute interstitial nephritis and chronic kidney disease; a possible link with dementia. (K 1094)
Drug interactionsInhibits CYP2C19, so it reduces activation of clopidogrel; avoid together. (L 741; K 1095) May raise levels of warfarin, diazepam and phenytoin; reduces absorption of ketoconazole, itraconazole, digoxin and atazanavir. (K 1095)
PregnancyNot teratogenic in animals, but safety in pregnancy is not established. (K 1094)
Q.2 (7) 6 marks

A 16-year-old boy (55 kg) presented to the emergency department of DHQ hospital with history of shortness of breath which is not controlled by the inhalers. How will you control acute attack of asthma in this patient?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 41 (Drugs for Disorders of the Respiratory System), pp. 721–726 (management algorithm, Fig. 41.3, p. 722) · Katzung Ch. 20 (Drugs Used in Asthma), pp. 360, 362

1. Assess and grade the attack

  • Take a history and examine him. Look for life-threatening signs: drowsy, confused, silent chest. If present, transfer urgently to an acute care facility, giving a short-acting β2 agonist (SABA), oxygen and a systemic steroid on the way. (L 722)
  • An attack not controlled by his own inhalers is managed as moderate-to-severe, with close assessment and repeated measurement of lung function. (L 722; K 360)

2. Treatment step by step (Lippincott Fig. 41.3)

StepDrugs and doses (calculated for 55 kg)
Initial treatment (L 722)
  • Oxygen.
  • Salbutamol nebulized 2.5 mg (5 mg/ml solution) every 20 min × 3, or by MDI with spacer 4 puffs (100 mcg each) at 2–3 min intervals, repeated every 20 min × 3.
  • or Adrenaline 1:1000, 0.01 ml/kg SC every 20 min × 3: 55 × 0.01 = 0.55 ml, above the maximum, so give 0.3 ml.
  • Steroid: prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg (55–110 mg) in divided doses, or hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg IV stat (220 mg), or methylprednisolone 40 mg every 6 h.
Reassess every 30–60 min. Poor response (L 722)Repeat the initial treatment; add ipratropium bromide inhalation (mixed or alternated with salbutamol); oxygen; oral prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg.
Still poor (L 722)Bronchodilator 1–2 hourly and ipratropium 8-hourly; continue steroids; give one dose of magnesium sulfate.
Still poor (L 722; K 360)One dose of magnesium sulfate or aminophylline, whichever was not used, or refer to a higher center. If respiratory failure develops: intubation and mechanical ventilation. (K 360)
Good response at any stage (L 722)“Last in, first out”: stop aminophylline in 12–24 h, then ipratropium in the next 12–24 h, reduce salbutamol to 4–6 hourly, plan discharge.
Discharge (L 721–724)Inhaled or oral salbutamol 6-hourly; oral steroid for 3–5 days, stopped without tapering; review his controller (inhaled corticosteroid), inhaler technique and triggers.

Katzung's steroid dose for a severe attack is prednisone or methylprednisolone 0.5 mg/kg every 6–12 h (about 27 mg for him). (K 360)

3. How the drugs work

  • Salbutamol: β2 agonist; relaxes airway smooth muscle within 5–30 min; tremor, tachycardia and hypokalemia are its side effects. (L 721; K 362)
  • Ipratropium: blocks vagally mediated bronchoconstriction and mucus secretion; adds benefit to a SABA in the emergency department. (L 726)
  • Corticosteroids: inhibit phospholipase A2; reduce airway inflammation and mucosal edema. (L 724)
  • Aminophylline (theophylline): non-selective phosphodiesterase inhibitor → ↑ cAMP; narrow therapeutic window; overdose causes seizures and arrhythmias. (L 726)

Q.2 (9) 6 marks

Give mechanism of action and adverse effects of fluoroquinolones.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 31 (Quinolones, Folic Acid Antagonists, and Urinary Tract Antiseptics), pp. 537–540 · Katzung Ch. 46 (Sulfonamides, Trimethoprim, & Quinolones), pp. 837, 839–840

Drugs: nalidixic acid (1st generation); ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin (2nd); levofloxacin, gatifloxacin (3rd); moxifloxacin, gemifloxacin, delafloxacin (4th). (L 537–538)

Mechanism of action

  1. Fluoroquinolones enter the bacterium through porin channels in the cell wall. (L 537)
  2. They inhibit the two bacterial type II topoisomerases: DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV. (L 537; K 837)
  1. DNA gyrase cuts double-stranded DNA and adds negative supercoils to relieve the twisting ahead of the replication fork. Blocking it prevents the relaxation of supercoiled DNA needed for replication and transcription. (L 537; K 837)
  2. Topoisomerase IV separates the two daughter chromosomes after replication; blocking it stops cell division. (L 537; K 837)
  3. The drug–enzyme complex stops the cut DNA from being joined again → permanent chromosome breaks → cell lysis. Bactericidal. (L 537)
  4. Main target: DNA gyrase in Gram-negative bacteria, topoisomerase IV in Gram-positive bacteria. Killing depends on AUC/MIC. (L 537)
  5. Resistance: mutations in the target enzymes (gyrA, parC), fewer porins, efflux pumps. (L 539)

Adverse effects

SystemAdverse effects
Gut (most common)Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea; more C. difficile infection. (L 539, 537; K 839)
CNSHeadache, dizziness, insomnia; boxed warning for hallucinations, anxiety, confusion and seizures. (L 539; K 839)
NervesPeripheral neuropathy, which may last months to years or be permanent. (L 539; K 840)

Q.2 (10) 6 marks

What is the mechanism of action, clinical uses and side effects of Phenytoin?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 12 (Drugs for Epilepsy), pp. 249, 252 (Fig. 12.13) · Katzung Ch. 24 (Antiseizure Drugs), pp. 414, 418–419, 433–435

Mechanism of action

  • Phenytoin blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. It binds the channel in its inactivated state and slows its recovery. (L 249; K 414)
  • During a seizure neurons fire at high frequency, so the channels pass through the inactivated state often and the block builds up (use-dependent block). High-frequency firing stops; normal firing is little affected. (K 414)
  • Less action-potential firing means less transmitter release at synapses. (K 414)

Clinical uses

  • Focal (partial) seizures and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, alone or with other drugs. (L 249, 252; K 418)
  • Status epilepticus, after a benzodiazepine; IV fosphenytoin (a prodrug) is preferred. (L 249, 252; K 433)
  • Not for absence or myoclonic seizures, which it may worsen. (K 418)

Side effects

GroupSide effects
CNS (dose-related)Nystagmus (early sign), diplopia and ataxia (the commonest reasons to reduce the dose), sedation, slurred speech, tremor, confusion; chronic toxicity can damage the cerebellum; very high levels may even increase seizures. (L 249; K 419)
CosmeticGingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, acne, coarsening of facial features. (L 249; K 419)
Nerve and bonePeripheral neuropathy (reduced deep tendon reflexes); osteoporosis and osteomalacia (abnormal vitamin D metabolism). (L 249; K 419)
BloodLow folate, megaloblastic anemia; rarely agranulocytosis. (K 419)
HypersensitivityRash, fever, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, lymphadenopathy. (L 252; K 419)
InjectionNever IM (tissue necrosis); IV can cause “purple glove syndrome”. (L 249; K 418)
PregnancyRisk of congenital malformations is not zero. (K 434)
  • Kinetics: saturation (zero-order) kinetics within the therapeutic range, so a small dose increase can cause a large rise in level and toxicity; therapeutic range 10–20 mcg/mL. (L 249; K 418–419)
  • Interactions: induces CYP2C, CYP3A and UGT enzymes, so it lowers levels of carbamazepine, valproate, steroids, cyclosporine and oral contraceptives; valproate displaces it from plasma proteins. (L 249, 252; K 418)

Q.3 15 marks (assumed; none printed) Repeated topic

Explain the role of Iontophoresis in physical therapy? What types of drugs are used in these procedures? Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of this procedure.

Source: Katzung Ch. 7, p. 115 (the word only); Ch. 3, pp. 47–48; Ch. 26 (Local Anesthetics), p. 471; Ch. 61 (Dermatologic Pharmacology), pp. 1068–1069 · Lippincott Ch. 1, pp. 29, 31–32 (Fig. 1.19), 34; Ch. 13, pp. 274, 277; Ch. 26, pp. 472, 474; Ch. 40, pp. 698–699

Note: iontophoresis is a physical-therapy technique that neither book covers. Katzung names it once (p. 115), as a way of applying a drug directly to tissue in experiments; Lippincott does not mention it. So the definition, the mechanism, the drug list and most of the advantages and disadvantages are tagged. The books do cover the skin as a barrier, the transdermal and topical routes, and the drugs themselves, and those points are cited.

1. What iontophoresis is

  • Iontophoresis is the delivery of an ionized (charged) drug through intact skin into the tissue below, using a low-level direct (galvanic) electric current. [not in book]
  • It is a way of applying a drug to the skin. Like the transdermal route, it avoids the hepatic first-pass effect. (L 32; K 48)

2. Role in physical therapy

  • Used to treat local, superficial musculoskeletal conditions: tendinitis, bursitis, epicondylitis, plantar fasciitis and other soft-tissue inflammation. [not in book]
  • Also for local pain relief and anesthesia, calcium deposits (calcific tendinitis), scar tissue and adhesions, excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis) and slow-healing wounds. [not in book]
  • It lets the therapist give an anti-inflammatory or anesthetic drug at the painful site without a needle, as part of a rehabilitation programme with exercise. [not in book]

3. Why a current is needed: the skin barrier

  • Skin is a series of diffusion barriers. A drug must leave its vehicle, cross the stratum corneum, pass through the deeper epidermis into the dermis, and is then absorbed into the blood. (K 1068–1069, Fig. 61–1)
  • A drug crosses membranes much more readily when it is uncharged; the charged (ionized) form crosses poorly. (L 34) That is why a transdermal patch needs a highly lipophilic drug. (L 32)
  • Penetration varies with the site (face, axilla and scalp are far more permeable than the forearm), the concentration gradient, the vehicle and occlusion. (K 1068) Special mixtures such as EMLA (lidocaine + prilocaine) are made to get a local anesthetic through the keratinized layer. (K 471)
  • Iontophoresis solves the charge problem by using the drug's own charge to drive it in. [not in book]

4. How it works (mechanism of delivery)

  • Like charges repel (electrorepulsion). The drug solution is placed under the electrode with the same charge as the drug ion (the active electrode): positive ions under the anode (+), negative ions under the cathode (−). The current pushes the ions away from the electrode and into the skin. [not in book]
  • A second, larger dispersive electrode elsewhere on the skin completes the circuit. [not in book]
  • Electro-osmosis: the current also moves water through the skin, which carries drug with it. Ions pass mainly through sweat ducts and hair follicles. [not in book]
  • Dose = current × time (mA·min); commonly about 40–80 mA·min per session. [not in book]
  • Once through the stratum corneum the drug spreads into the dermis and local tissue and is partly absorbed into the blood, as for any drug applied to skin. (K 1069)

5. Types of drugs used

Requirements: the drug must be ionized, water-soluble, of small molecular size, and effective in small amounts at a local site. [not in book] This is the opposite of a transdermal patch, which needs a highly lipophilic drug. (L 32)

(b) Mechanism of action

  • Phospholipase A2 frees arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids. The cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway turns it into prostaglandins, thromboxanes and prostacyclin. (L 694)
  • COX-1 is constitutive: it maintains gastric protection, platelet aggregation, vascular homeostasis and kidney function. COX-2 is induced at sites of inflammation (by TNF-α and IL-1) and is also normally present in brain, kidney and bone. (L 694)
  • NSAIDs inhibit COX, the first step of prostanoid synthesis, so prostaglandin production falls. (L 696)
  • Aspirin acetylates COX irreversibly; the other NSAIDs are reversible inhibitors. (L 697, Fig. 40.8)
  • COX-2 inhibition gives the anti-inflammatory and analgesic actions; COX-1 inhibition gives most adverse effects (and aspirin's protection against cardiovascular events). (L 696)
  • They do not block the lipoxygenase pathway, so leukotriene formation continues. (L 701)

The three actions (L 697)

  • Anti-inflammatory: fewer prostaglandins to drive inflammation; but they do not stop arthritis progressing.
  • Analgesic: less PGE2, which normally sensitizes nerve endings to bradykinin and histamine; used for mild-to-moderate pain.
  • Antipyretic: less PGE2 in the hypothalamus, so the raised temperature set point returns toward normal; no effect on normal body temperature.

(c) Clinical uses

  • Anti-inflammatory and analgesic: osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, gout; headache, arthralgia, myalgia, dysmenorrhea; mild-to-moderate musculoskeletal pain. With opioids for cancer pain, allowing lower opioid doses. (L 697) Ketorolac for more severe pain, short term only. (L 697)
  • Fever: aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen. Avoid aspirin under 19 years with viral illness (chickenpox, influenza) because of Reye's syndrome. (L 698)
  • Cardiovascular (low-dose aspirin, 75–162 mg): prevents recurrent myocardial infarction, transient ischemic attack and stroke; also in acute myocardial infarction. (L 698) Aspirin is also used in rheumatic fever. (L 698)
  • Topical, important in physical therapy: diclofenac, nimesulide, piroxicam and ibuprofen gels for sports injuries, sprains, osteoarthritis and tenosynovitis, with very low systemic exposure; methyl salicylate as a counterirritant in liniments; salicylic acid for acne, corns, calluses and warts. (L 698)
  • Migraine: abortive treatment of mild-to-moderate attacks. (L 699) Eye: ketorolac and flurbiprofen drops for allergic conjunctivitis and after eye surgery. (L 698)
  • Celecoxib: rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis and acute pain; etoricoxib also for ankylosing spondylitis. (L 706)
  • Long-term low-dose aspirin is linked with less colon cancer. (K 645)

(d) Adverse effects

Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. (L 699) Adverse effects are similar for all NSAIDs. (K 645)
