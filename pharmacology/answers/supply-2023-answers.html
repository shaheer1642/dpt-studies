Short Q.1 5 marks (assumed)

Define bioavailability. Enumerate factors effecting it.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 1 (General Pharmacology, Pharmacotherapeutics, and Pharmacokinetics), pp. 34–37 (Fig. 1.25) · Katzung Ch. 3 (Pharmacokinetics & Pharmacodynamics), pp. 47–48 (Table 3–3)

Definition

  • Bioavailability is the rate and extent to which an administered drug reaches the systemic circulation. (L 36) Katzung: the fraction of unchanged drug reaching the systemic circulation after administration by any route. (K 47)
  • Example: if 100 mg of a drug is given orally and 70 mg is absorbed unchanged, bioavailability is 0.7 (70%). (L 36)
  • After IV injection 100% of the drug enters the circulation, so IV bioavailability is 100% by definition. (L 36; K 47)

Measuring bioavailability: compare the area under the curve (AUC) after an oral dose with the AUC after the same dose IV. Redrawn from Lippincott Fig. 1.25 (L 36).

Bioavailability (F) = AUC (oral) ÷ AUC (IV) × 100%
  • Knowing bioavailability is needed to calculate doses for non-intravenous routes. (L 36)

Factors affecting bioavailability

A. Related to the drug

  1. First-pass (presystemic) metabolism: an oral drug passes through the gut wall and portal circulation to the liver before reaching the systemic circulation, and may be metabolized on the way. More than 90% of nitroglycerin is cleared this way, so it is given sublingually, transdermally or IV. (L 36) Highly extracted drugs: morphine (oral bioavailability about 33%), lidocaine, verapamil, propranolol, isoniazid. (K 48)
  2. Solubility: very hydrophilic drugs (e.g. atenolol) cannot cross lipid membranes; extremely lipophilic drugs (e.g. acyclovir) are insoluble in body water. A drug must be largely lipophilic with some water solubility. (L 36; K 47)
  3. Chemical instability: penicillin G is destroyed by gastric acid; insulin by gut enzymes. (L 37)
  4. Formulation: particle size, salt form, crystal polymorphism, enteric coating, excipients; crushing or splitting tablets changes absorption. (L 37)
  5. Ionization (pH and pKa): only the uncharged form crosses membranes easily. (L 34–35)

B. Related to the patient

  1. Blood flow at the absorption site: intestine more than stomach; shock reduces absorption from subcutaneous sites. (L 35)
  2. Surface area: the intestine has about 1000 times the surface of the stomach. (L 35)
  3. Contact time: severe diarrhea reduces absorption; food slows gastric emptying. (L 35)
  4. P-glycoprotein pumps drug from gut cells back into the lumen; grapefruit juice inhibits it and gut-wall metabolism, so absorption rises. (L 35; K 47)
  5. Liver function and hepatic blood flow: in cirrhosis with portosystemic shunting, availability of highly extracted drugs rises greatly. (K 48)

C. Route of administration (K 47, Table 3–3): IV 100%; IM and SC 75 to ≤100%; oral 5 to <100%; rectal 30 to <100%; inhalation 5 to <100%; transdermal 80 to ≤100%.

Short Q.2 5 marks (assumed)

Give differences between zero and first order elimination with help of diagram and 3 examples in each case

Source: Lippincott Ch. 1, pp. 42–43 (Figs. 1.30, 1.31), 48; Ch. 12, p. 249; Ch. 14, p. 285; Ch. 40, p. 699; Ch. 46, p. 802; Ch. 48, p. 828 · Katzung Ch. 3, pp. 45–46; Ch. 24, pp. 418–419, 422; Ch. 27, p. 487

Definitions

  • First-order elimination: a constant fraction of the drug is eliminated per unit time; the rate is directly proportional to the drug concentration. (L 43; K 45)
  • Zero-order elimination: a constant amount of the drug is eliminated per unit time, whatever the concentration, because the enzyme is saturated. (L 43)

A. Concentration against time (ordinary scale). First order falls exponentially; plotted on a log scale it becomes a straight line (L 42, Fig. 1.30). Zero order falls by a fixed amount each hour (L 43).

B. Rate of elimination against dose. Redrawn from Lippincott Fig. 1.31 (L 43).

Short Q.3 5 marks (assumed)

A young child of 6 years has been given atropine before his eye examination by a doctor.
A) what are pharmacological effects of atropine on eye?
B) what are other clinical uses of anti muscarinic agents?

Source: Lippincott Ch. 5 (Cholinergic Antagonists), pp. 135–139 (Figs. 5.4, 5.7) · Katzung Ch. 8 (Cholinoceptor-Blocking Drugs), pp. 127, 130–132 (Table 8–2)

Atropine is a tertiary amine belladonna alkaloid. It binds competitively to muscarinic receptors and stops acetylcholine binding. After topical use in the eye its effects may last for days. (L 135)

A) Effects of atropine on the eye

  1. Mydriasis (dilated pupil): the pupillary constrictor muscle needs muscarinic activation; when this is blocked, the sympathetic dilator acts unopposed. (K 127; L 135)
  2. Loss of the light reflex: the pupil does not respond to light. (L 135)
  1. Cycloplegia: the ciliary muscle is paralysed, so accommodation is lost and the eye cannot focus for near vision. (L 135; K 127)
  2. Photophobia and blurred vision from the fixed, dilated pupil and cycloplegia; may last up to 2 weeks. (L 135)
  3. Less tear secretion: dry, “sandy” eyes. (K 127; L 137)
  4. Rise in intraocular pressure: in angle-closure glaucoma it may rise dangerously, so atropine is contraindicated there. (L 135, 139; K 127)

Why the doctor used it: mydriasis plus cycloplegia lets refractive error be measured without the eye accommodating, and makes the retina easier to examine. In young children atropine's greater efficacy is sometimes needed, but the risk of poisoning is higher; an ointment reduces drug loss down the nasolacrimal duct. (L 136; K 130) Children are very sensitive, especially to a rapid rise in body temperature. (L 137) Mydriasis lasts 7–14 days, so tropicamide or cyclopentolate (6–24 h) are now often preferred. (L 136, 138) (Katzung gives atropine's effect as 5–6 days. K 130, Table 8–2)

B) Other clinical uses of antimuscarinic agents

Short Q.5 5 marks (assumed)

A patient had more than 2 attacks of gout. Name the drugs used to decrease the production of uric acid in this patient, along with mechanism of action of these drugs.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 40 (Anti-inflammatory, Antipyretic, and Analgesic Agents), pp. 712–715 · Katzung Ch. 36 (NSAIDs, DMARDs, Nonopioid Analgesics, & Drugs Used in Gout), pp. 661–662 (Fig. 36–7)

  • A patient with more than two attacks a year is a candidate for urate-lowering therapy. (L 712–713)
  • Uric acid is made from purines: hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid. The last two steps use the enzyme xanthine oxidase. (L 714; K 662)
Purines→ Hypoxanthinexanthine oxidase→✕ Xanthinexanthine oxidase→✕ Uric acid

✕ = blocked by allopurinol and febuxostat (L 714, Fig. 40.20; K 662, Fig. 36–7)

Drugs that decrease uric acid production: xanthine oxidase inhibitors

Allopurinol and febuxostat are the first-line urate-lowering drugs. (L 713)

1. Allopurinol
  • Mechanism: a purine analog (an isomer of hypoxanthine). It competitively inhibits xanthine oxidase, blocking the last two steps of uric acid synthesis. (L 714; K 661–662)
  • Its metabolite alloxanthine (oxypurinol) also inhibits xanthine oxidase and has a half-life of 15–18 h, so one dose a day works. (L 714; K 662)
  • Result: plasma urate falls; the more soluble xanthine and hypoxanthine rise instead. (K 662)
  • Dose: start 50–100 mg/day; increase until serum uric acid is below 6 mg/dL (usually 300–400 mg/day). (K 662)
  • Adverse effects: skin rash and hypersensitivity (most common); may precipitate gout early; GI upset; rarely marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity. (L 714; K 662)
  • Interaction: reduce azathioprine dose by about 75%. (K 662)
2. Febuxostat
  • Mechanism: a non-purine, potent and selective xanthine oxidase inhibitor; reduces formation of xanthine and uric acid without affecting other enzymes of purine or pyrimidine metabolism. (K 662; L 714)
  • Dose: 10–120 mg once daily. Needs less dose change in kidney disease than allopurinol. (L 714)
  • Adverse effects: abnormal liver tests, diarrhea, headache; caution in heart disease or stroke. (L 714)

Related: pegloticase (recombinant uricase) lowers uric acid by converting it to allantoin, not by reducing its production; IV, for patients failing standard therapy. (L 715)

Precaution: starting urate-lowering therapy can trigger an attack, so give low-dose colchicine, an NSAID or a corticosteroid with it for at least 6 months. (L 713)

Short Q.6 5 marks (assumed)

What do u understand by DMARDs? Enumerate them

Source: Lippincott Ch. 40, pp. 697, 708–712 · Katzung Ch. 36, pp. 649–657

What DMARDs are

  • DMARDs = disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs. They are used in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) to slow the course of the disease, induce remission, and prevent further destruction of joints and involved tissues. (L 708)
  • They modify the disease itself. NSAIDs, by contrast, reduce inflammation but neither stop progression nor induce remission. (K 649; L 697)
  • Their effect takes 2 weeks to 6 months to appear. (K 649)
  • Start as soon as possible after diagnosis; methotrexate is generally preferred first. If one drug is not enough: a combination of traditional DMARDs, or a TNF inhibitor or non-TNF biologic. NSAIDs or glucocorticoids may be added for their anti-inflammatory action. (L 708)
  • Biologics are large-molecule drugs, usually proteins made by recombinant DNA technology. (K 649)

Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are the drugs of choice for long-term control of asthma; oral or IV steroids are added for exacerbations or severe asthma. (L 724) Adverse effects depend on route, dose and duration. (L 475)

  • In children, ICS slow growth by about 1 cm in the first year only; little effect on adult height. Chronic use may add to osteoporosis and cataracts; high doses can mildly suppress the adrenal. (K 355)

Short Q.8 5 marks (assumed)

Give triple regimen of H pylori eradication with doses.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 42 (Gastrointestinal and Antiemetic Drugs), pp. 737–739 (Fig. 42.4) · Katzung Ch. 62 (Drugs Used in the Treatment of Gastrointestinal Diseases), p. 1093

  • Helicobacter pylori and NSAIDs are the two main causes of peptic ulcer. Eradicating H. pylori heals ulcers rapidly; recurrence falls to less than 15% a year, against 60–100% with acid-lowering drugs alone. (L 737)
  • The most effective regimens combine two antibiotics and a proton-pump inhibitor (PPI). (K 1093)

Standard triple therapy (first line, preferred)

DrugDoseHow often
PPI (see doses below)Standard doseTwice daily
Amoxicillin1 gTwice daily
Clarithromycin500 mgTwice daily
Duration 7–10 days (up to 14 days); eradication rate 70–85%. (L 739, Fig. 42.4)

PPI doses (each twice daily, 15–30 minutes before meals): omeprazole 20 mg, or lansoprazole 30 mg, or pantoprazole 40 mg, or rabeprazole 20 mg, or esomeprazole 40 mg (esomeprazole may be once daily). (L 739)

Example prescription: omeprazole 20 mg + amoxicillin 1 g + clarithromycin 500 mg, all twice daily for 14 days. (from L 739)

Penicillin-allergic patient

  • Replace amoxicillin with metronidazole 500 mg twice daily: PPI + clarithromycin 500 mg + metronidazole 500 mg, twice daily for 10–14 days (70–85%). (L 738–739)

Katzung's version

  • A 14-day “triple therapy”: PPI twice daily, clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, and either amoxicillin 1 g twice daily or metronidazole 500 mg twice daily. (K 1093)
  • After the antibiotics, continue the PPI once daily for a total of 4–6 weeks to make sure the ulcer heals. (K 1093)

Points to add

  • Why a PPI: raising gastric pH lowers the minimum inhibitory concentration of the antibiotics against H. pylori; PPIs also have a minor direct antibacterial effect. (K 1093)
  • When triple therapy is chosen: where clarithromycin resistance is low and the patient has had no macrolide before. (L 738) Rising clarithromycin resistance is why quadruple therapy is now first line in some patients (see Q.9). (K 1093)
  • Confirm eradication after treatment. (L 738)

Short Q.10 (typed) 5 marks (assumed) Also in an earlier paper

write down mechanism of action of aminoglycosides with specific adverse effects.

Source: Lippincott Ch. 30 (Protein Synthesis Inhibitors), pp. 525–527 · Katzung Ch. 45 (Aminoglycosides & Spectinomycin), pp. 826–829

Drugs: gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, streptomycin, neomycin (L 526–527); kanamycin (K 829).

Mechanism of action

  1. Entry, step 1: passive diffusion through porin channels in the outer membrane of the bacterium. (L 525; K 826)
  2. Entry, step 2: active transport across the cytoplasmic membrane by an oxygen-dependent process driven by the electrochemical gradient. So they do not work in anaerobic conditions or at low pH. (L 525–526; K 826)
  1. Synergy: cell-wall drugs such as penicillin or vancomycin increase aminoglycoside entry. (K 826; L 526)
  2. Target: they bind the 30S ribosomal subunit. (L 526; K 826)
  3. Effect: protein synthesis is inhibited in three ways: (a) interference with the initiation complex; (b) misreading of mRNA, so wrong amino acids make nonfunctional proteins; (c) breakup of polysomes into nonfunctional monosomes. The overall effect is irreversible and kills the cell. (K 826–827; L 526)
  4. Killing pattern: bactericidal and concentration dependent (target peak 8–10 times the MIC), with a post-antibiotic effect, so high-dose, extended-interval dosing is used. (L 526)

Specific adverse effects

EffectDetails
OtotoxicityDrug accumulates in the endolymph and perilymph. Auditory: tinnitus, high-frequency hearing loss, possibly irreversible deafness. Vestibular: vertigo, ataxia, loss of balance. Worse with loop diuretics or cisplatin. Most auditory: neomycin, kanamycin, amikacin. Most vestibular: streptomycin, gentamicin. (L 527; K 829)
NephrotoxicityDrug retained in proximal tubular cells: mild reversible impairment up to acute tubular necrosis; rising creatinine. Most nephrotoxic: neomycin, tobramycin, gentamicin. Worse with loop diuretics, vancomycin, amphotericin. (L 527; K 829)
Neuromuscular paralysisCurare-like block with high doses given quickly or with neuromuscular blockers; respiratory paralysis. Myasthenia gravis patients at special risk. Reversed by calcium gluconate or neostigmine. (L 527; K 829)
AllergyContact dermatitis with topical neomycin. (L 527)
PregnancyCross the placenta; fetal ototoxicity, so contraindicated. (L 527)

Toxicity is more likely with therapy over 5 days, high doses, old age and renal insufficiency, so plasma levels are monitored. (L 527; K 829)

Short Q.10 (photo) “up to 5 lines” each

Write short answers of the following up to 5 lines maximum:

Source: Lippincott Ch. 5, p. 142; Ch. 9, pp. 197–198; Ch. 30, p. 527; Ch. 32, pp. 551–552; Ch. 40, pp. 699, 701; Ch. 42, pp. 744–745; Ch. 46, p. 805 · Katzung Ch. 27, p. 483; Ch. 33, pp. 594, 596; Ch. 36, p. 661; Ch. 45, p. 829; Ch. 47, pp. 842–843; Ch. 57, p. 1032; Ch. 62, pp. 1105–1106

a) What is the effect of using combination of Aminoglycoside plus tubocurarine

  • The neuromuscular block is enhanced and prolonged: risk of respiratory paralysis and prolonged apnea. (L 142, 527; K 829)
  • Why: aminoglycosides reduce acetylcholine release from the motor nerve by competing with calcium (blocking presynaptic calcium channels), so they add to tubocurarine's block of the nicotinic receptor. (L 142; K 483)
  • Myasthenia gravis patients are at particular risk. Reversed by calcium gluconate or neostigmine. (L 527)

b) Why deferoxamine is prescribed to a patient of thalassemia major receiving multiple blood transfusions?

  • Many transfusions over a long time cause chronic iron overload (iron deposited in heart, liver, pancreas), which can cause organ failure and death. (K 596)
  • The body has no mechanism to excrete iron, and in refractory anemias such as thalassemia major the iron cannot be removed by phlebotomy. (K 594, 596)
  • Deferoxamine is an iron chelator: it binds iron to form ferrioxamine, which is excreted in urine. Given IM or IV (poor oral absorption). (L 805; K 1032)

c) Name first line anti-tuberculous drugs.

First-line drugTypical adult dose (K 843)
1. Isoniazid300 mg/day
2. Rifampin (rifabutin or rifapentine may replace it)600 mg/day
3. Pyrazinamide25 mg/kg/day
4. Ethambutol15–25 mg/kg/day

Preferred for high efficacy and acceptable toxicity; all four for 2 months, then isoniazid and rifampin for 4 months. (L 551–552; K 842)

d) Why low dose of aspirin is not prescribed in patients of hyperuricemia?

  • At low doses (2.6 g/day or less) aspirin causes renal retention of uric acid; it becomes uricosuric only above 3.6 g/day. (K 661)
  • Salicylate is secreted into urine and affects uric acid excretion, so aspirin should be avoided in gout. (L 699)
  • It also decreases urate excretion with probenecid. (L 701, Fig. 40.14) Result: serum uric acid rises and gout may be precipitated.

e) What is the mechanism of action of benzodiazepines?

  • Target: the GABAA receptor, five subunits around a chloride channel. GABA opens the channel; Cl− enters and hyperpolarizes the neuron. (L 197–198)
  • Benzodiazepines bind their own high-affinity site at the α–γ subunit interface (not the GABA site) and increase the frequency of channel opening by GABA, enhancing inhibition. (L 198)

f) Enumerate dopamine antagonists used as antiemetic drugs.

They block dopamine D2 receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone. (L 744–745)

  1. Phenothiazines: prochlorperazine, promethazine, thiethylperazine (L 744; K 1105)
  2. Butyrophenones: droperidol, haloperidol (L 745; K 1106)
  3. Substituted benzamides: metoclopramide, trimethobenzamide (L 745; K 1106)
  4. Domperidone (crosses the blood–brain barrier poorly) (L 745)
  5. Olanzapine (thienobenzodiazepine; also blocks 5-HT receptors) (K 1105–1106)

Main adverse effect of the central dopamine antagonists: extrapyramidal (restlessness, dystonias, parkinsonism). (K 1106)

The books do cover the skin as a barrier, what increases drug penetration through skin, the topical and transdermal routes, drugs that work through the skin, and the advantages and disadvantages of each route; those points are cited. Everything specific to ultrasound is tagged. The photo is printed as Q.3 and part (c) is cut off; it is answered as the advantages and disadvantages of phonophoresis compared with other routes of drug administration.

What phonophoresis is

  • Phonophoresis (sonophoresis) is the use of therapeutic ultrasound to drive a drug applied to the skin through the intact skin into the tissues underneath. [not in book]
  • It is a form of delivery through the skin. The books describe the topical route (applied where a local effect is wanted) and the transdermal route (applied to skin to get systemic effects, usually by a patch). (L 31)

a) Mechanism of delivery of drugs

1. The barrier the drug must cross
  • Skin is a complex series of diffusion barriers. The drug must leave its vehicle, diffuse through the stratum corneum, partition into the stratum spinosum, dermis and subcutaneous tissue, and then either bind receptors locally or be absorbed into the bloodstream. (K 1068–1069, Fig. 61–1)
  • Normally only highly lipophilic drugs, effective in small daily doses, pass well. (L 32)
  • What normally increases penetration: higher concentration gradient, a vehicle that hydrates the stratum corneum, occlusion (plastic wrap gives about a tenfold rise in hydrocortisone absorption), more permeable regions (scrotum, face, axilla, scalp more than forearm), and inflamed skin. (K 1068–1069, 1080)

Skin layers and the drug's path, after Katzung Fig. 61–1 (K 1069). The transducer, coupling gel and the idea that ultrasound pushes drug down are [not in book].

2. How ultrasound drives the drug in
  • Procedure: the drug is mixed into a coupling medium (gel or cream) that transmits ultrasound, spread on the skin, and the transducer head is moved over it, usually at 1 MHz (deeper) or 3 MHz (superficial), continuous or pulsed, for about 5–10 minutes. [not in book]
  • Non-thermal (mechanical) effects: cavitation, where tiny gas bubbles form and oscillate in the gel and stratum corneum, disorganizing its lipid layers and opening temporary channels; and acoustic streaming, a one-way flow that pushes drug molecules along. [not in book]
  • Thermal effects: heating raises the kinetic energy of drug molecules, dilates hair follicles and sweat ducts, and increases local blood flow and cell membrane permeability. [not in book]
  • Pathways: through the lipid spaces between corneocytes, and through hair follicles and sweat ducts. [not in book]
3. After the barrier
  • The drug moves into the dermis and deeper tissues, where it binds receptors locally; some is absorbed into the blood and has systemic effects. (K 1069)
  • Topical NSAIDs penetrate up to the dermis, and systemic exposure after topical application is very low. (L 698)
  • Skin acts as a reservoir, so the “local half-life” can be long. (K 1068)

b) Drugs that can be delivered

What the drug must be like: lipophilic, effective in small daily doses; drugs of high molecular weight or poor lipid solubility are unsuitable for skin delivery. (L 32) For phonophoresis it must also be in a medium that transmits ultrasound; many thick creams and ointments transmit it poorly. [not in book]

c) Advantages and disadvantages compared with other routes

AdvantagesDisadvantages
  • Bypasses first-pass metabolism, unlike the oral route, where drug may be metabolized before it reaches the circulation. (L 32, 36; K 47–48)
  • Avoids the gut: no destruction by stomach acid, no effect of food on absorption, no gastric irritation; useful when a patient cannot take drugs by mouth. (L 29, 32, 698)
  • Painless, no needle: avoids injection problems: pain, necrosis with irritant drugs, need for strict asepsis, intramuscular hemorrhage in patients on anticoagulants. (L 32, Fig. 1.19)
  • High concentration at the site, low elsewhere, so fewer systemic side effects, as with other topical routes. (K 47; L 698)
  • Drug need not be ionized and no electric current is used, unlike iontophoresis. [not in book]
  • The heating and mechanical effects of ultrasound may themselves help healing, so drug and physical treatment are given together. [not in book]
  • Reaches deeper tissues (tendon, bursa) than simple rubbing-in of a cream. [not in book]
  • Only some drugs: must be lipophilic and effective in small daily doses. (L 32)
  • Variable absorption: depends on the skin at the site and the drug's lipid solubility; varies between people. (L 31, 698; K 1068)
  • Slow: delayed delivery to the site of action, so not for emergencies, where IV gives immediate effects. (L 32, Fig. 1.19)
  • Skin reactions: irritation or allergy, as with patches (L 32); topical steroids can cause skin atrophy, and over large areas HPA suppression and growth retardation in children. (K 1081)
  • The dose actually delivered is unknown and not standardized; research results are mixed. [not in book]
  • Needs an ultrasound machine and a trained therapist; time-consuming, repeated sessions. [not in book]
  • Ultrasound contraindications: over a malignancy, pregnant uterus, eyes, gonads, growing epiphyses, pacemaker, deep vein thrombosis, infection, areas with poor sensation; risk of burns or periosteal pain if misused. [not in book]

Summary for comparison: the oral route is the safest, most common, convenient and economical route (L 32); injections give rapid and complete systemic delivery (IV bioavailability 100%) (K 47); phonophoresis gives local delivery to superficial musculoskeletal tissues without a needle and without first-pass loss, at the cost of small, uncertain doses.

Long Q.2 15 marks (assumed) Also in an earlier paper

Enumerate skeletal muscle relaxants. give mechanism of action and adverse effects. what are the clinical indications in my hugh [sic] skeletal muscle relaxants are used?

Skeletal muscle relaxants form two therapeutic groups: neuromuscular blockers, used in surgery and the ICU to produce paralysis (they act at the motor end plate and have no CNS action), and spasmolytics and antispasmodics, used to reduce spasticity and muscle spasm (traditionally “centrally acting”). Dantrolene acts directly on muscle. (K 474, 485)

D. Direct-acting (on the muscle or nerve terminal) (K 487–489)

2. Mechanism of action

A. Nondepolarizing blockers (tubocurarine, rocuronium, etc.)
  • Low doses: compete with acetylcholine (ACh) at nicotinic receptors of the motor end plate without stimulating them, so the membrane cannot depolarize and the muscle does not contract (flaccid paralysis). (L 140; K 478)
  • High doses: also block the ion channel of the end plate, so the block is deeper and harder to reverse. (L 141; K 478)
  • Order of paralysis: small fast muscles of face and eye first, then fingers, limbs, neck and trunk, then intercostals, and the diaphragm last; recovery is in reverse. (L 141; K 481)
  • Reversal: cholinesterase inhibitors (neostigmine, edrophonium) raise ACh at the junction; sugammadex binds rocuronium and vecuronium. (L 140–141; K 484)
B. Depolarizing blocker (succinylcholine)
  • Attaches to the nicotinic receptor and depolarizes it like ACh, but resists acetylcholinesterase and stays bound. (L 142)
  • Phase I: sodium channels open, the end plate depolarizes, and transient fasciculations occur, followed by flaccid paralysis. Phase II: with time the membrane repolarizes but becomes resistant to depolarization. (L 142)
  • Hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase, so the block lasts less than 10 minutes. (K 477, 481)
C. Spasmolytics and antispasmodics
DrugMechanism
BaclofenGABAB agonist: closes presynaptic calcium channels and increases postsynaptic K+ conductance, causing hyperpolarization; less release of excitatory transmitters in brain and spinal cord, so Ia afferents, interneurons and motor neurons are suppressed. May also reduce substance P release (pain). (K 485–486)
DiazepamFacilitates GABA at GABAA receptors (more frequent chloride channel opening); at high doses relaxes spasticity, probably by increasing presynaptic inhibition in the spinal cord. (L 198; K 485)
Tizanidineα2-adrenoceptor agonist (clonidine congener) in the spinal cord: reinforces presynaptic and postsynaptic inhibition; inhibits pain transmission in the dorsal horn. (K 487)
CyclobenzaprinePoorly understood; acts mainly at the brainstem, reducing the muscle stretch reflex. Related to tricyclic antidepressants. (K 488–489)
D. Direct-acting drugs
  • Dantrolene: binds the ryanodine receptor (RyR1) calcium channel of the sarcoplasmic reticulum and blocks calcium release, so excitation–contraction coupling and actin–myosin interaction are reduced. Heart and smooth muscle are hardly affected (they use RyR2). (K 487–488; L 268)
  • Botulinum toxin: its light chain (a zinc protease) cleaves SNAP-25 or synaptobrevin, so ACh vesicles cannot fuse and ACh is not released; local chemodenervation and flaccid paralysis lasting weeks to months (about 2–3 months). (K 487, 489)

4. Clinical indications

Neuromuscular blockers (L 140, 143; K 484–485, 489)

  • Surgical relaxation, especially abdominal and chest surgery; allow lighter anaesthesia and faster recovery.
  • Endotracheal intubation, including rapid sequence intubation: succinylcholine (fastest onset) or rocuronium (fastest nondepolarizing).
  • Control of ventilation in the ICU (e.g. cisatracurium, whose elimination does not depend on liver or kidney).
  • To reduce the muscular part of convulsions: status epilepticus, local anaesthetic toxicity, electroconvulsive therapy (succinylcholine).

Spasmolytics: spasticity from upper motor neuron lesions (K 485–489)

  • Cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, stroke, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: baclofen (oral or intrathecal for severe spasticity), tizanidine, diazepam, oral dantrolene.
  • Baclofen weakens muscle less than dantrolene, and tizanidine causes markedly less weakness; useful where strength is needed for rehabilitation. (K 486–487)

Antispasmodics: acute local muscle spasm (K 474, 488–489)

  • Spasm after local trauma or muscle strain (cyclobenzaprine, diazepam); chronic back pain and painful fibromyalgic conditions. Cyclobenzaprine does not help spasm from cerebral palsy or spinal cord injury.

Special uses

  • Dantrolene IV: malignant hyperthermia (1 mg/kg, repeated up to 10 mg/kg) (K 488; L 268); also neuroleptic malignant syndrome. (L 232)
  • Botulinum toxin: upper and lower limb spasticity in cerebral palsy and multiple sclerosis, cervical dystonia, overactive bladder, chronic migraine, hyperhidrosis, facial wrinkles. (K 487, 489)

Background: in RA the immune system attacks the synovium. T lymphocytes recruit macrophages, which release the cytokines TNF-α and interleukin-1 (IL-1); these make synovial cells proliferate and produce collagenase, which breaks down cartilage and stimulates bone resorption. B lymphocytes produce rheumatoid factor and keep the inflammation going. (L 693, 709) Treatment aims to reduce inflammation and pain and to halt or slow disease progression. (L 693)

(c) Biological agents: adverse effects

Common to all biologic DMARDs (L 710; K 650, 656)

  • Serious infections: tuberculosis (reactivation of latent TB), opportunistic fungal infections, sepsis. Screen for TB before starting.
  • Hepatitis B reactivation; screen before starting.
  • No live vaccines during treatment.
  • Do not combine a TNF-α inhibitor with a non-TNF biologic: risk of severe infection.

TNF-α inhibitors

  • Injection-site reactions (20–40%); antidrug antibodies in about 17%, which can reduce efficacy. (K 656)
  • Can cause or worsen heart failure. (L 710; K 656)
  • Cancer: Lippincott reports increased risk of lymphoma and other cancers (L 710); Katzung reports more skin cancers, including melanoma, but no clear increase in lymphoma or solid tumours (K 656).
  • Antinuclear and anti-dsDNA antibodies; drug-induced lupus very rare. (K 656)
  • Adalimumab: headache, nausea, rash, injection-site reaction, agranulocytosis. (L 710)
  • Infliximab: infusion reactions (fever, chills, pruritus, urticaria). (L 711)
  • Golimumab: raised liver enzymes. (L 711)

Non-TNF biologics

  • Abatacept: infusion reactions, headache, upper respiratory and urinary infections, nausea; rare anaphylaxis. (L 711; K 650)
  • Rituximab: infusion reactions (urticaria, hypotension, angioedema), mostly with the first infusion, so methylprednisolone is given 30 minutes before; rash; falling immunoglobulins; serious infections up to a year after the last dose; hepatitis B reactivation; cytopenias. (L 711; K 652)
  • Tocilizumab and sarilumab: raised liver tests, hyperlipidemia, neutropenia, hypertension, infusion and injection-site reactions; GI perforation reported. (L 711–712; K 653)
  • Tofacitinib: infections (upper respiratory, urinary, opportunistic; screen for TB), anemia (hemoglobin must be above 9 g/dL to start), low lymphocytes and neutrophils, raised LDL and HDL, new malignancy, GI perforation. (L 712; K 657)

How these fit together for her

  1. Start a traditional DMARD early, usually methotrexate (response in 3–6 weeks), with an NSAID or low-dose glucocorticoid as a bridge for symptoms. (L 708–709)
  2. If the response is inadequate, add a TNF-α inhibitor or a non-TNF biologic, alone or with methotrexate; response may be seen within 2 weeks. (L 710)
  3. If one TNF-α inhibitor fails: add a traditional DMARD, or switch to a non-TNF biologic or another TNF-α inhibitor. (L 710) Tofacitinib is kept for failure of, or intolerance to, methotrexate and other agents. (L 712)
  4. Before and during biologics: screen for latent TB and hepatitis B (K 650, 656); for methotrexate, check liver tests, blood counts and signs of infection (L 708); with rituximab, check blood counts every 2–4 months. (K 652)
